# Candidates — The Best Meal You Ever Had

Judge brief criteria, most important first: (1) stays on the subject
of the best meal you ever had; (2) makes the meal concrete — named
dishes, a place, a texture, a smell or one specific moment rather
than general praise; (3) feels true to one person's memory rather
than a stock line anyone could have written; (4) one clean sentence
that reads aloud well, with fresh wording and no cliche; (5) lands
with an image, a turn or an ending that stays with the reader after
a single reading.

All candidates below are original, written for this bounty. Only
public submission titles were visible, never entry texts, and no
candidate copies a title's wording.

## The six candidates

| # | Chars | Line |
|---|-------|------|
| C | 190 | The best meal I ever had was my sister's lontong sayur from a plastic bag in a hospital corridor at 2am, coconut broth soaking the rice cake while we waited to hear if our father would wake. |
| A | 185 | The best meal I ever had was salted fish my mother fried on a borrowed kerosene burner after the flood took our stove, eaten with hot rice on the stairs, chili oil still on our fingers. |
| B | 168 | The best meal I ever had was a 5000-rupiah bowl of bakso eaten standing outside my first job interview, broth scalding my tongue while I rehearsed answers I never used. |
| E | 185 | The best meal I ever had was plain rice and a fried egg my grandmother slid across the table the morning I failed my exam, yolk unbroken, as if one good thing could still be kept whole. |
| F | 181 | The best meal I ever had was half a stranger's nasi bungkus on a night bus to Surabaya, sambal burning through banana leaf and newspaper while the driver sang off-key to stay awake. |
| D | 183 | The best meal I ever had was charcoal sate my uncle grilled on a paint-can stove the night our lights went out, peanut sauce gritty with ash because nobody wanted to wait for perfect. |

Each candidate is a single sentence with one full stop, at most 200
characters, and opens on the subject so criterion (1) is met before
any detail is weighed.

## Local judging (pairwise by the brief's criteria, in order)

- **C — PRIMARY (submitted).** (1) Explicit best-meal frame. (2) The
  most concrete set in the group: a named dish, a plastic bag, a
  hospital corridor at 2am, coconut broth soaking rice cake — place,
  vessel, texture and moment in one breath. (3) The truest to one
  memory: a sister sharing food while the family waits on a father's
  waking could belong to no stock line. (4) Reads aloud in one clean
  arc with no cliche phrase. (5) The ending stays: the meal is
  remembered because of what the waiting meant, not because the food
  was praised.
- **A — runner-up.** Nearly as concrete (borrowed burner, stairs,
  chili oil on fingers) and the flood gives it real stakes. Loses to
  C on (3) and (5): hardship cooking is a more familiar shape than
  the hospital wait, and its last image is sensory where C's is
  human.
- **E — third.** The unbroken yolk as the one thing kept whole is
  the prettiest single image here. Loses to C on (4): grandmother
  plus a consoling plate is the field's most crowded shape, so it
  reads fresher in isolation than it will in a pairwise heat.
- **B.** The 5000-rupiah price and the unused rehearsed answers are
  sharply personal, and it is the tightest line. Loses to C on (2):
  one scalded tongue is thinner than C's layered detail, and the
  meal itself matters less than the interview around it.
- **F.** Vivid and warm, with the singing driver as a lovely tail.
  Loses to C on (3): a stranger's kindness on a bus is a story many
  travellers could tell, while C's corridor belongs to one family.
- **D.** Gritty ash in the peanut sauce is an honest texture. Loses
  to C on (5): nobody wanting to wait for perfect is a shrug where
  C ends on a held breath.
